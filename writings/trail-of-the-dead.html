The Trail of the Dead

Cedric Phillips

“I had a dream, which was not all a dream.

The bright sun was extinguish'd…”

We packed the swimsuits in the car because every sign pointed to a hot day. The sun was beating down in Whistler, the forecast promised a high of 30°C, and it’s always hotter down in Lillooet than it is in Whistler. This was besides the fact that due to the smell of burnt trunks and branches flooding the valleys, the Air Quality Health Index that morning was a 9 – but surely, I thought, if the trees felt it hot enough to go up in smoke, the lake would be warm too. In the end not everything turns out the way you expect it to and the swimsuits often stay in the car, so you have to think about what you learned from your trip to the lake instead. To do so is a choice – an attempt to impose “a narrative line upon disparate images”, to ‘aestheticize’ – but it is the choice I will always make. A day remembered as ‘on the road’ is better than one remembered as ‘failed lake day’. So let me tell you what I remember of our journey to Pavilion Lake.

We could start in Pemberton since there is nothing extraordinary about the road from Whistler to Pemberton, but Whistler is where I live, so for me it has to start there. But it would also be unfair to my friend to say that, because she was visiting from an alien place – Toronto – and said simply “Wow,” when we turned and Wedge loomed over my Honda Fit on the 99. We passed the shooting range on the way up Suicide Hill, and despite my friend’s wishes, did not indulge. We were going swimming, after all. On the way down into Pemberton the Signal Hill fire had gone out, and my friend pointed out the horses grazing on either side of the road. The sight was idyllic, through it was hard to reconcile the looming shadows of the mountains with the overly optimistic image of equestrians galloping over rolling plains. In any case the horses were a good omen, proven as we soon stopped for coffee, and a pastry each. It was hard to say which would be more essential.

Not too long after Pemberton is Mount Currie, and then Mount Currie Indian Reserve 1, heralded by lower speed limits. Here is the Hitching Post Motel – justification for our earlier horses. Here are the messages about park closures written on the back of signs reading “Staying Home Saves Lives – Follow COVID-19 safety protocols – Kukwstum̓úlhkál’ap”. Here is where you begin to wish you had passed that campervan a little earlier. Here is the heart of the Líl’wat Nation, two hours from Lillooet. Here is the last frontier of picturesque Coast Mountains tourism ads – dark blue water, green fields, Thuja plicata, snow-dappled peaks.

When I was a child, we often drove the Duffey Lake Road. Of this time I still have two particular images: unpaved corners of raw gravel, and my parents starving me beforehand due to my tendency to vomit. That day I held the steering wheel of my nimble little Honda.

“Why do you always accelerate in the corners?” my friend asked at a certain point.

“I mean – it’s more effective, right?” I began.

But it seemed after that that I could not really explain how, which I knew would be the next question.

“I can try to not, if you’d like,” I said instead.

I cannot remember what we decided but I know that I kept accelerating in the corners anyway, though more deliberately thereafter. I will admit that until you’re past Joffre Lakes there is nothing really to see on the Duffey except perhaps the sign warning about grizzly bears, and owing to the skies choked with smoke, there was less to see than usual. In this part the lack of cell service frustrated my friend, who was trying to put together a playlist that would seem relevant to the drive. The landscape seemed to demand something else – perhaps an audiobook – but it was hard to know what would be the right story to hear told.

In Líl’wat legend, there were Transformers who roamed the land, “for the purpose of putting things to rights in the world, and killing everything that was bad”. It is hard to find evidence of this one way or another – a particularly phallic spire of rock is not necessarily proof of a mythological penis, in my mind – but it is hard to deny that this is a land of transformation, constructed so precisely that many of the touches seem to be deliberate, unjustifiably bordering on the artificial. After Joffre, transformation begins. You can see the avalanche paths like bright clawmarks in the phthalo green mountainsides. You notice them on the opposite side of the valley, then above you, and then you feel a bit more glad for the warm weather. My friend noticed the log jam at the far side of Duffey Lake, and I noticed that it had not come undone yet. Given that the lake itself is called Teq (“log jam”) by the Líl’wat, I suppose it never will.

The signs along the road descending from Duffey Lake seemed to become more abstract, and I began to wonder if we were lost, addled by the smoke. “Cottonwood”, “Cinnamon”, they proclaimed. Old Líl’wat stories of Duffey Lake sometimes mention it as a haunt of wetscín – lizards who eat the intestines of people. We passed “Lizard Creek” but saw no lizards. Was anything as it should be? The ground was getting drier, and so were the trees. The rocks were getting warmer in tone, and someone was crumbling them down into sand, down into Cayoosh Creek. The Adventurer in front of us was going 20 km/h under the speed limit. The car with Alberta plates behind us was going 20 km/h over in the straight sections and 20 km/h under in the corners, but thankfully there were lots of corners. The air was getting hotter. The smoke was getting thicker. Rockslide debris had pushed itself to the edge of the road. Either the land was being painted in yellower tones or the sunlight was. Across the valley my friend pointed out a dainty white waterfall that tucks itself in a comfortable pleat of dark mountainside, trickling down from some glacier lost in the haze. My map says it is called Phair Falls but the name doesn’t seem to reveal anything.

Already the land was almost a moonscape, and then we saw it: “
LILLOOET: GUARANTEED RUGGED”, and after a few corners, Seton Lake came into view below. The Bridge River Power Project that gave rise to Seton Lake – three dams, four powerhouses, seven transformers – did not come into view, because the water is sucked from the northern Bridge River valley through a tunnel bored into Mission Mountain, and from there transformed into electricity. There is a curve after that, shaped in a U-Turn sometimes said to resemble the Lululemon logo, but the concept does not stick. There is no space in the mind for both Chip Wilson’s $69,878,000 Vancouver mansion over the salt water of English Bay and the Bridge River Power Project, and before we could think about it too much, it was down, over the one-lane wooden bridges, past the mobile homes, and then it was Lillooet.

“Guaranteed Rugged”. In fact it is hard to think of Lillooet and to think of anything as guaranteed. One imagines that insurance companies would flee at the sight of it, a tinderbox left on a high shelf above the hearth. All around Ponderosa pines jut out of the landscape like the sticks I used for ‘trees’ in my bad dioramas in third grade. Needles blanket the ground, or maybe my younger self had used bottle green paint. In the centre of town, further risks reveal themselves. The implacable mountains jutting above threaten landslides. The Fraser below reaches its fishy hands up and claws at the homes – all vinyl siding and Spruce-Pine-Fir – perched on its sandy bank. Rapids just in view upstream indicate the river’s feisty character, promising great floods if not fed a sufficient diet of motorhomes.

Up Main Street and into the center of town we went, and then peering through the haze across the Fraser brought us yet another revelation. Our eyes tracked across the Bridge of the Twenty-Three Camels – named for the ungulates ill-advisedly imported to haul freight in 1862, perhaps not such a bad idea after all, I thought in the heat – and landed on the impeccably green vineyards of Fort Berens Estate Winery. Just in front, a Eurocopter hovered in the air, bucket dangling, going for a big drink in the Fraser – but no, mirage, it landed on the ground instead, and we were tricked by our expectations. In any case I was and am not a fire chief so I had no comment.

It was lunchtime. There are several restaurants in Lillooet, but we were not there to eat in Lillooet. We were there to eat at a Lillooet restaurant, which meant we had to go to Totem Chinese. We tried to park in the shade but were foiled by the sun pressing its way through the tenebrous sky. The backdrop was a series of yellow ridges climbing indistinctly into orange-grey, punctuated by a single sign: “MOTEL”. Inside the Totem, we learned that it is a “LICENCED DINING ROOM”, and that we could order à la carte or partake in the buffet. The buffet was being actively patronized by a decent flock of customers, yet soon we were seated on cream vinyl seat cushions and were leafing through the menu. The TV was on in the corner: “Fire badly damages apartment building”, “More Mexican firefighters arrive in BC”, “Advancing marmot research”, vague communiqués that seemed to have little to do with the mythos of Totem Chinese, told instead through mementos behind the maneki-neko-topped register: an array of operating certificates, grad photos, a drum ornamented with a formline salmon. We were by the window but there was no point in looking outside. My friend, at last, said it:“It’s like we’ve been transported to another realm, or something. And now we’re in a Western. Where are the cowboys.”

“Like, because of the restaurant?” I asked.

Johnny came over. Johnny is the owner and seems to be perpetually smiling, and has thirty-four thousand followers on Instagram. He wanted our order.

“Uh, broccoli with chicken, Szechuan beef, two rice, please.”

“That’s gonna be a lot of food,” he said. “You good with that?”

We were good with that. My friend got directions to the washroom, and soon Johnny came to check on me again:

“What you ordered, it’s gonna have a lot of vegetables. You good with that?”

At a certain point there’s only so much of your life you can control, but you have to appreciate the residua of hospitality where they seem to exist. When my friend returned I showed her Johnny’s latest Instagram post: “Another beautiful fucking Smokey day in Lillooet!”

Totem is not the place for coffee. As outsiders we relied on Google Maps, and soon, per my friend, found ourselves in another dimension altogether at the DeOro Coffee Lounge. The front room almost conjured up a tavern, and I got my latte to go from the smiling barista while my friend slipped away further into the Hotel DeOro.

“It was fucking crazy,” she said as soon as we were out the door, “like, it was this big empty room, it felt like no one had been in there for years.”

She showed me a photo. The Hotel DeOro reception desk has three clocks: London, New York, Lillooet. A mini dome security camera stares down at the doorway. The charcoal carpet has a dizzying geometric pattern. An apparently random section of the wall is draped in a metallic gold foil fringe curtain. Flyers bearing QR codes on the corkboard read: “Raise your spiritual vibrations! Softly sing, ‘HU-U-U-U’ (sounds like the word hue). Chant with reverence. Let it open your heart. Scan to explore more.” and “HU – Connect with the God Current”.

“Those flyers are insane,” I said.

“Oh – well, yeah. But the room was just – it was someplace else.”

It was time to leave Lillooet.

We had managed to get across the Fraser before fire or flood could hit, which was to be expected and felt like a stroke of good luck anyway. This side is the great trickster Coyote’s side, legend says, but instead the sign said “HWY 12 - South, HWY 99 - North”. On the 99 we could see the vines of Fort Berens Estate Winery and the army of sprinklers that waters them.

“It’s kinda grotesque, isn’t it?” my friend asked.“What, the winery?” I checked. I was prepared to disagree.

“Yeah. It just looks like it doesn’t belong here.”

I could have talked about the proud British Columbian wine tradition, or argued that all of the crops here use a ton of water; you just don’t notice it because they’re not as green. Instead I started to say “you know, it gets pretty cold here too,” but by then we were almost above the canyon; you could see a little white motorboat fighting its way up the Fraser rapids, and it didn’t seem to be relevant anymore.

The people in Pavilion are said to be descendants of Coyote, and between Lillooet and Pavilion is the Keatley Creek archaeological site, a village of one hundred and nineteen pit-houses inhabited starting eight thousand years ago and vacated by the thirteenth century. The village held at least a thousand people at its peak, when it was part of a network of villages that contained one of the largest Indigenous populations in Canada. We did not stop to visit, and in fact almost no one does, but we could easily have, could have taken a short walk through brush and ranchland, seen the inhabitant goats. Would we have witnessed an ancient world or would we have witnessed one hundred and nineteen circular depressions in the ground? It seemed like anything could happen out there, and it seemed as we drove up to Pavilion Indian Reserve 1 that the Canadian flag was no longer red.

“Is that a green Canadian flag?” my friend asked.

“I mean, yeah, I guess,” I say, but then we turned the corner.

“Oh. It’s a weed flag.”

Someone else was blazing it up, too, because just over the ridge behind Pavilion the Pear Lake wildfire had burned just over 133,000 hectares. Other than the thickening smoke, I had no evidence of this except the BC Wildfire Service map, but the road behind Pavilion was closed in corroboration. In any case, the Ts’kw’aylaxw First Nation’s signs proclaimed “NO VISITORS ALLOWED DUE TO COVID-19”, and Pavilion Lake was farther still, so we continued along into the canyon below the great conflagration above.

Marble Canyon is a mosaic of natural figures, or perhaps more accurately a canvas onto which fantastic narratives can be painted. The canyon is made of limestone. It lies within the territory of the Ts’kw’aylaxw, who are mixed with their northeastern Secwépemc neighbours. A jagged pinnacle of rock, K’lpálekw – Coyote’s Penis – watches over the cerulean lakes, and it is said that those who climb it will die soon after. I said we were going to Pavilion Lake, but I lied. As the story goes, Pavilion Lake was the haunt of a powerful skunk who was in the habit of killing people, but was defeated by a hero who emptied its scent bag into the lake, causing its deep blue colour. Farting Lake – Np’étkwe7ten. A sign told us it holds living microbialites.

We stopped at Crown Lake instead, and, braving the hot midday air, changed into our swimsuits – we were going swimming, after all. As we descended slowly to the pebbly shore, the lake seemed drier than usual. The stones dug into the soles of our feet, yet we could not seem to make ourselves wade in. I dipped my toes in and it felt perilously cold. Suddenly a strong wind was blowing from the north, or maybe it had been blowing all along. A shimmering portal appeared on the lake, or maybe it didn’t. The air had shifted, though the sky remained clouded with smoke. The point is that there was a chill. We walked along the shore desultorily until it got grassy; we spotted a snake, a few baby ducks. The chill did not appear to affect them, but they paddled away from us. We could not swim.

We had exhausted every option and the only thing to do was return to the Honda, get in the sweltering seats, get scalded by the seatbelt, and start driving. From Marble Canyon you can keep going, east into Secwépemc land and into Cache Creek, but the only appropriate move seems to go west – west on the Líl’wat trail of the dead. It is said that this trail – wide, deep, covered in red ochre – is walked facing into the sunset. It never rains on the trail, so it is always very dusty and dry. At the end of the trail, the land of souls is “like fall... like Indian summer.” September was eons away, and so we must have had lots of trail left, but somehow we ended up back in Whistler.

We went out for dinner at the restaurant where I worked that night, and my manager asked what we had done with our day.

“Lillooet?” he said, carefully taking away our wine glasses. “Aw, you should have told me! – next time you go, go to Fort Berens, give me a call – you’ll get the royal treatment.”

It seemed like we had missed the point, but maybe we just couldn’t see it.
